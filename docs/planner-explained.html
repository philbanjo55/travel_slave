How the Planner Works

Twice a day the planner downloads 51 slightly different versions of the forecast, scores every one of your rated shots every 15 minutes in each version, and builds the best day from the hotel and back. The next night it checks what the sky actually did. Two versions run side by side: the original, which trusts the forecast's cloud numbers, and a learning one, which corrects them by what really happened.

The nightly loop

From forecast to plan, and back

Step 1 · score a shot

One shot, 51 possible skies

For every rated stop, the planner looks at its starred viewpoint every 15 minutes. In each of the 51 forecast versions it works out a score from 0 to 250: is the sun likely to break through the low and middle cloud, is the light coming from the direction you asked for (front, side, back or soft), and does rain, wind, fog or low cloud on the hills spoil it. A score of 150 or more counts as a good shot.

Step 2 · plan the day

Building the best day

Starting at the night's hotel, the planner tries adding stops one at a time, with real drive times between them, and keeps the 150 most promising half-built days at each step (300 when it plans several days together). Every day ends back at a hotel.

A day's value is the average across the 51 skies plus half the average of the worst 11. So a day that is great in most versions but a washout in the rest scores lower than one that is solid in all of them.

Rules it follows

  • Tiers weigh the shots: GOD counts 10, S 5, A 2, B and C 1.
  • The same subject counts once: two views of one peak only add the improvement.
  • Drives under an hour are free; each hour beyond that on one leg costs a point.
  • Stops from 5:00 AM to 11:30 PM; GOD and S stops from 3:45 AM when the odds are 50% or better.
  • Nailed shots drop out; shots you got only count if a visit could beat them.

It keeps the top 5 different plans, the safest one, and "my day": your own stops at your own times, scored the same way, so you can see what the planner would change.

The difference between the two

How often is it really clear?

The original planner assumes that when the forecast shows little low and middle cloud, the sun will be out. The learning planner replaces that with what actually happened when the forecast said the same thing: first from years of airport records, then corrected every day by the last 30 days.

How it's doing

Promised vs delivered

Every plan made the evening before is checked the next night. Each visit is scored at its planned time against the nearest truth: your webcam tap first, then Claude's reading of the camera, then the airport report. Hidden hills score 0, clear but no sun when you wanted sun 0.4, a good shot 1.

"Random hour" is the same truth at every daylight hour that day: what you'd get by just turning up at any time. Each planner needs to beat it to earn its keep.

Is the forecast itself any good?

Separately, every forecast hour that has passed is checked against the airports and webcams. Skill above zero means the forecast beat simply guessing the usual rate; below zero means guessing would have done better.

Where this stands

  • Learning is more honest. Its promised chances are much closer to what happened, and its error score is lower (0.32 vs 0.37 on best plans).
  • Neither picks better times yet. Both land at about the random-hour level. Three grey Faroes days give them little to choose between.
  • Too few visits to be sure. About 30 checked visits per planner. A real difference needs a few hundred, which the two practice trips reach in early November.
